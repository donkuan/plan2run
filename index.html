Race result conditions

From James Copeland's Norwegian Singles Method: heat by temperature and dew point (whichever is worse), hills as climb and descent per km, wind for an out-and-back or loop (head- and tailwind) unless you pick one direction. Exposed means seafront or track; light cover is trees or a park (wind effect −20%); urban or forest −40%. Route “Loop” covers loops and out-and-backs. For a point-to-point race, the climb matched by descent counts as rolling terrain and the rest as a net uphill or downhill. Gravel or dirt adds 2.5%.

Race predictions use Daniels and Gilbert's VDOT equations; threshold pace is about 88% of VO2max and easy pace 60-70%. Treat them as starting points: let the HR cap rule a sub-threshold session, run at altitude by effort, and reset the VDOT after every race. Two rules from autumn 2026: no single week far above your normal volume, and no all-out race within 4 weeks of a marathon.

The weekly structure

  • 1× heavy barbell session: maximum strength for hamstrings, glutes and hip flexors (Session A).
  • 2× prehab: straight after the lighter running days. Your PT work lives here (Session B).

Based on Andreas Almgren's routine from his book: 4–5 barbell exercises with a clear purpose, done well, for general explosiveness and stability in the hamstrings, glutes and hip flexors. He goes lighter with more reps far from a race, heavier closer to it, and lighter but explosive in season. Sets, reps and the doses below are starting points; adjust them with your PT.

Session A: Main strength

1× per week, heavy. Keep it to 4–5 focused exercises, no extra fluff. Load and reps follow the periodization table.

  1. Hang cleans (from the knees)General explosiveness.
  2. Squats, single-leg squat with ball, or hip thrustsPick one per session.
  3. Bulgarian split squats (PT variation)Hold a weight and add a slight rotation toward the bent front leg. Hits the glutes hard while the foot and post tib have to stabilise.
  4. Single-leg good morningsFrom the PT list. Hips square, back leg straight.
  5. Step-ups with hip flexionStep onto a box and drive the opposite knee up. Cables can add resistance.

Session B: Prehab

2× per week, straight after easy runs. The PT glute and post tib exercises, cut down to the most effective movements that don't overlap.

  1. Heel raise with ball between ankles3 × 15, 3 s downPost tib focus. The best isolation for the posterior tibialis: squeezing the ball stops the ankles rolling outward, so the post tib has to do the work. Replaces the standard soleus and isometric calf holds.
  2. Eccentric step-downs3 × 10 per legGlute medius focus. Stand on a box, keep the other leg straight and lower yourself slowly. Functional for running and makes the glute medius stabilise the knee. Replaces the step-up variations from the PT list.
  3. Single-leg sweep3 × 10 per legPost tib and glute. Stand on one leg with a soft knee, keep the arch lifted and the knee over the foot, and slowly sweep the free leg backward, to the side and front.
  4. Standing banded hip abduction3 × 15 per legGlute activation. Pilates band around the ankles, feet turned in (pigeon-toed), lift the leg out to the side. Replaces the clamshell variations.

Injury: signal or noise

What you feelWhat to do
Sudden, new and quite painfulStop immediately
New during a session and slowly getting worseStop the session early
Still there 2–3 sessions laterAdjust training. A couple of rest days and some treatment are often enough
Very mild, gone by the next session, or moving around the bodyNoise: train as normal

Illness

SituationWhat to do
FeverNo training, non-negotiable. Rest as much as possible
Sore throatUsually a cold on its way: treat it like one and rest
Resting HR clearly up overnightEarly warning that a cold is coming
Fever goneWait at least one more day before training. Longer if other symptoms remain, like a heavy cough
No fever, dry throat or slight sniffleTrain as usual, carefully. Postpone a really hard session by a day. In base training it hardly matters whether you rest, run easy or do a threshold session

Cross-training when injured

The type of cross-training matters very little. Choose what is least mentally taxing and most enjoyable, and rotate if you like variety. To get back to racing quickly, train harder and as close to running as possible, but rushing an injury is always risky.

  • Your normal routine, but on a bike, cross-trainer or in the water
  • Lots of threshold work, several days in a row, on a cross-trainer
  • Very high intensity, several days in a row, on a bike or cross-trainer
  • Train like a cyclist, following a cycling coach's programme
  • Lots of cycling, all at low intensity
